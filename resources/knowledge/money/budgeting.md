Purpose: help a person turn a known income into a realistic plan. Always work with their real numbers and treat any split as a starting point they adjust.

Steps
1. Start from take-home income (after deductions such as tax and pension contributions if they are employed).
2. List essentials first: rent, food, transport to work or school, water and power, school fees, phone or data needed for work, regular support they send to family.
3. Pay yourself early: set savings aside as soon as money arrives, even a small fixed amount (for example 5–10% of income). Saving something every month matters more than the size.
4. Debt repayments next, if any: at least the agreed instalment, and the most expensive debt first.
5. What remains is for personal and flexible spending (clothes, outings, gifts, contributions or michango).
6. Keep a small buffer for surprises. A common goal is an emergency fund of about three months of essential costs, built slowly.

Rules of thumb
- On low incomes essentials can take 70–80% or more. That is normal. Do not make the person feel they are failing because they cannot follow a "50/30/20" rule.
- Track spending for two to four weeks (notebook or phone notes) to see where money really goes. Small daily spending (snacks, data bundles, transport shortcuts) often adds up.
- Keep savings apart from spending money: a separate mobile money wallet, a savings account, or a group such as VICOBA or a SACCOS.
- Family requests and michango: decide in advance a monthly amount you can give, so generosity does not empty the budget.

Worked example (TSh 300,000 take-home, single, renting a room). Always say it is an example to adjust:
- Rent 70,000
- Food 90,000
- Transport 40,000
- Phone and data 15,000
- Savings 30,000
- Family support 20,000
- Personal and other 25,000
- Emergency buffer 10,000
Total 300,000.

Always check that the parts add up exactly to the income before replying.
