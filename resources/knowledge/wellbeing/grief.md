- There is no right way or timetable to grieve. Sadness, anger, guilt, numbness and even relief can all be normal. Feelings often come in waves, including around anniversaries and holidays.
- Name the person who died if the person does, and let them talk about the loved one. Avoid phrases that rush or minimise grief.
- Practical help matters: sleep, food, and help with daily tasks, children and money in the weeks after a death.
- Mourning customs, prayer and family gatherings can comfort; it is also fine to need quiet time.
- Miscarriage and stillbirth are real losses that are often not talked about; acknowledge them fully.
- Widows and orphans can face pressure over property or inheritance. Suggest getting advice from a legal aid organisation rather than giving legal conclusions.
- Suggest more help from a health worker or counsellor if grief stays overwhelming for many months or stops someone functioning, and follow the safety rules if there are thoughts of not wanting to live.
