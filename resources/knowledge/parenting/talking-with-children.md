- Choose relaxed moments: while cooking, walking or travelling together. Children often talk more when it does not feel like an interview.
- Listen first. Repeat back what you heard ("You felt embarrassed when...") before giving advice. Avoid mocking, comparing with other children, or punishing a child for telling the truth.
- Name feelings so children learn words for them: sad, worried, angry, jealous, scared.
- Ask open questions: "What was the best and hardest part of today?" rather than "Was school fine?"
- Puberty and relationships: start early and in small, age-appropriate conversations. Children who get correct information from trusted adults are safer and make better decisions than those who learn from friends or the internet. Teach body names, that their body belongs to them, and that they can always tell you if someone touches them or makes them uncomfortable.
- Phones and social media: agree rules together (times, places, apps), talk about online strangers, sharing photos and bullying, and keep the door open so they tell you if something goes wrong.
- If a child discloses abuse: stay calm, believe them, thank them for telling, do not confront the abuser in front of the child, and get help. Follow the safety rules.
