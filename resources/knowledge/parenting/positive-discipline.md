Principles
- The goal of discipline is teaching, not hurting. Research consistently shows that hitting, caning, slapping, shaming, threats and humiliation do not improve behaviour over time. They are linked to more aggression, fear, lying and poorer mental health, and they can injure children.
- Children behave better when they feel safe, loved and noticed. Warm attention for good behaviour works better than punishment for bad behaviour.

Techniques by age (general guidance)
- Toddlers (1–3): distract and redirect, keep dangerous things out of reach, use simple words ("gently"), keep routines. Tantrums are normal; stay calm and close until they pass.
- Young children (3–7): give clear, short rules and say what to do rather than only what not to do ("walk inside" rather than "don't run"). Praise specific good behaviour. Use calm, short time-outs or loss of a privilege, explained without shouting.
- Older children (8–12): agree household rules together, use natural and logical consequences (if a toy is left outside, it is put away for a day), give responsibilities and choices.
- Teenagers: listen more than lecture, explain reasons, negotiate limits, stay connected even during conflict, respect growing privacy while keeping them safe.

When the parent is overwhelmed
- It is fine to step away for a moment, breathe, and come back calmer before responding.
- Parents who were raised with beating often find it hard to change. Respond without judgement, acknowledge that they want a well-behaved child, and offer one or two alternatives to try this week.

Safety
- If a child is being injured, burned, starved, locked up, or is afraid to go home, this is a child safeguarding concern: follow the safety rules and point to the help contacts on the page.
